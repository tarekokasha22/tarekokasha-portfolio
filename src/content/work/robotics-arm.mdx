## Context

This was my final-year graduation project at the German International University in Cairo. I'm including it here not because it's the most commercially impressive thing I've built, but because it's where I learned how to think about systems — specifically, how every layer of a real system touches every other layer in ways that become obvious only after something breaks spectacularly at 11pm the night before a demonstration.

The brief: design and build a 6-degree-of-freedom robotic arm capable of identifying objects on a conveyor belt, sorting them by color and shape, and placing them in designated positions. The arm had to work in real time, using computer vision for object detection, without requiring human intervention once the task was initiated.

This is a well-understood academic problem. The implementation was not.

## Constraints

**No off-the-shelf arm.** The project required us to design and fabricate the physical arm, not just program a commercial unit. This added mechanical engineering to the scope. None of the four of us were mechanical engineers. We learned quickly.

**Real-time computer vision on constrained hardware.** The detection pipeline had to run fast enough to work with a conveyor belt running at a realistic speed. We had a Raspberry Pi 4 and a webcam. That's it.

**Precision under uncertainty.** Industrial robotic arms use precise servo encoders and known kinematics. Our arm used hobby servo motors with backlash, imprecise 3D-printed joints, and hand-calibrated kinematics. Getting reliable pick-and-place to work on a mechanically imperfect arm required compensation code that was, diplomatically, creative.

**Team of four, seven months, no budget.** The project cost approximately EGP 8,000 in parts, mostly from Cairo's electronics market in Ataba. We designed around what was available, not what was ideal.

## What I built

My contribution focused on the software stack: computer vision, inverse kinematics, and the control layer that connected perception to motion.

**Computer vision pipeline.** I used OpenCV for object detection, running on the Raspberry Pi. The pipeline captured frames from the webcam, applied color segmentation to identify objects by category, and used contour detection to estimate position on the conveyor. The challenge was lighting — the Ataba components included no consistent lighting, so I built adaptive white balancing and brightness normalization that adjusted to whatever the ambient light happened to be on a given day. In practice, this meant the system was robust enough to work under the fluorescent lights in the lab, the natural light from the windows, and the yellow sodium vapor lights in the hallway when we accidentally tested it there at midnight.

**Inverse kinematics solver.** Given a target position in 3D space (from the computer vision output), the IK solver calculated the joint angles required to reach that position. For a 6-DOF arm, this is not a trivial calculation — there are often multiple valid solutions, and some solutions require the arm to pass through positions it can't physically reach. I implemented a numerical IK solver using the Jacobian pseudo-inverse method, with collision avoidance that constrained solutions to physically reachable configurations. The solver ran in under 8ms per calculation on the Pi, which was the hard deadline for smooth motion.

**Servo control layer.** The calculated joint angles were translated to PWM signals for 6 servo motors, with timing calibrated against each motor's measured response characteristics. The backlash compensation code here is the part I'm least proud of technically and most proud of practically — it was a hack, it worked, and it worked consistently.

**Sorting logic.** Once the arm identified an object's position and category, the sorting logic determined the target placement position (red objects to zone A, blue to B, etc.), planned the pick-and-place sequence, and executed it with appropriate safety pauses. The whole sequence — identify, pick, move, place, return — took about 4 seconds per object.

## Decisions & tradeoffs

**OpenCV vs. a pre-trained neural network model.** We evaluated using a small YOLO model for detection. It would have been more robust to edge cases and unusual objects. We stuck with classical computer vision (color segmentation + contour detection) for two reasons: the Raspberry Pi's processing power made neural inference too slow for real-time use, and the objects we were sorting were controlled enough that the simpler approach worked reliably. If the objects had been variable or the lighting uncontrolled, we'd have needed the model.

**3D-printed joints vs. laser-cut acrylic.** We printed the joints in PLA plastic on the university's printer. The dimensional accuracy was ±0.3mm, which introduced backlash in every joint. Laser-cut acrylic would have been more precise and faster to manufacture. We chose 3D printing because it allowed more complex geometry in the joint design. In retrospect, simpler geometry with better dimensional accuracy would have been the right tradeoff.

**Python vs. C++ for the control software.** Python was the pragmatic choice — faster development, better OpenCV support, more familiar to the team. The performance constraints (8ms IK, real-time video) were achievable in Python with careful optimization. A C++ implementation would have run faster but would have taken 3x longer to write. For a 7-month academic project, Python was correct.

The project received a distinction grade. More importantly, it demonstrated that a team of 4 undergraduates with EGP 8,000 and 7 months could build something that worked — not perfectly, but consistently and measurably.

The computer vision pipeline ran reliably. The IK solver worked. The arm picked up objects and put them where we said it would. The part that impressed the examining committee wasn't the sophistication of any individual component — it was that all the components worked together without any of us having prior experience with any of the domains involved.

## What I'd do differently

Calibration took far longer than it should have. We spent the equivalent of 3 weeks total on calibrating servo positions, adjusting backlash compensation parameters, and re-tuning the IK constraints after mechanical changes. A proper calibration protocol — designed before the arm was built, with clear measurement procedures — would have halved that time. Instead, we figured it out as we went, which is a reasonable approach for learning and a terrible approach for shipping.

I'd also do more formal testing. We ran ~200 test pick-and-place cycles informally, but without systematic recording of failure modes and conditions. When something went wrong, we fixed it without always understanding why it had broken. A proper test matrix would have caught several recurring issues much earlier.
